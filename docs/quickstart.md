# Quickstart

This quickstart walks you from a blank page to a complete, send-ready invoice in about fifteen minutes. It assumes you have a service or product you are billing for, and a client who has agreed to pay.

## 1. Gather the inputs

Before you open any tool, collect the following on a single piece of paper:

1. **Your business identity**: legal name, trading name (if different), full address, contact email, and tax identifier if you have one.
2. **Your client's identity**: legal name and full billing address. Ask the client for their preferred billing email.
3. **What you are billing for**: a short description of each item or service, the quantity, and the agreed unit price.
4. **Payment terms** you agreed verbally or in a contract: typically "due on receipt", "net 14" or "net 30".
5. **Payment method**: bank transfer details, a payment-link URL, or both.

Most late or disputed invoices come from missing one of these inputs, not from formatting. Spend the five minutes to confirm them with the client before drafting.

## 2. Choose your tool

A usable invoice can be produced with any of:

- A word processor template.
- A spreadsheet template.
- A dedicated tool such as the online generator at [invoicegenerator.run](https://invoicegenerator.run/), which is the tool used for the worked examples in this guide.
- A full accounting application if you already use one.

For your first invoice, a simple online generator is usually the fastest path because it handles arithmetic and PDF export for you. You can graduate to accounting software once you have a steady stream of invoices.

## 3. Fill in the standard fields

In the order they typically appear:

1. **Invoice number** (see {doc}`workflow` for a numbering scheme that scales).
2. **Issue date** and **due date**.
3. **From** block with your business details.
4. **Bill to** block with the client's details.
5. **Line items** with description, quantity, unit price and line total.
6. **Subtotal**, **tax** (if applicable) and **total**.
7. **Payment instructions** with method and reference.
8. **Notes** field for thanking the client or stating any specific terms.

The {doc}`field-reference` chapter explains each of these fields in more detail and lists the common pitfalls.

## 4. Sanity-check before sending

Review the draft against this short checklist:

- Does the total match the sum of line items plus tax?
- Does the invoice number match the next number in your sequence and not duplicate a previous invoice?
- Are both addresses spelled correctly? Misspelt client names cause real delays in corporate accounts payable systems.
- Is the payment reference clearly visible, and does it match the invoice number?
- Is the due date a real date (not "end of month")?

## 5. Send and record

Send the invoice as a PDF attachment in an email rather than only as a link, so the client can archive it. Record the invoice in your own system with three fields: number, date sent, status ("open"). When the payment arrives, update the status to "paid" with the date received. This is the smallest possible accounts-receivable ledger and is usually enough for a freelancer.

The {doc}`workflow` chapter covers what to do when an invoice does not get paid by the due date.
