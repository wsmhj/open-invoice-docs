# Jurisdictions

Invoice requirements vary by country. This chapter summarises the practical differences between the United States, the European Union and the United Kingdom for a small business issuing invoices for services. It is general guidance and not a substitute for advice from a qualified accountant in your jurisdiction.

## United States

For most small businesses billing US clients, the minimum requirements are:

- A clear document title ("Invoice").
- Your business name and address.
- The client's name and address.
- An invoice number and issue date.
- A line-item description, quantity and amount.
- The total amount due.

There is no federal VAT in the US. Sales tax is a state and sometimes city matter, and applies primarily to tangible goods. Most service-based freelancers do not need to collect sales tax, but rules vary by state; check the rules for the state in which you are registered and for the state where the service is delivered if different.

If you have employees or contractors who issue you a W-9, you may need to send a 1099-NEC to each contractor by 31 January for the prior tax year. Your *outgoing* invoices to clients do not need to reference any of this.

## European Union

A VAT invoice in an EU member state typically requires:

- The word "Invoice".
- A unique sequential invoice number.
- The issue date and the date of supply if different.
- Your full business name, address and VAT number.
- The customer's full name, address and (for business customers in another EU country) VAT number.
- A description of the goods or services, quantity and unit price.
- The VAT rate applied, the amount of VAT in the currency of the invoice, and the total excluding and including VAT.
- The reason for any exemption or reverse charge, if applicable.

For business-to-business sales across EU borders, the reverse-charge mechanism usually applies: the seller does not charge VAT and the buyer accounts for it in their own VAT return. Note this with a phrase such as "Reverse charge: customer to account for VAT".

Several countries (Italy, Hungary, Spain, Portugal and others) require electronic invoicing through a central platform for many transactions. If you sell into those countries, check whether you need to register with the relevant platform and what additional fields are required.

## United Kingdom

UK invoices are similar to EU invoices but with two main differences after the UK left the EU VAT system:

- VAT registration is optional below an annual taxable turnover threshold (which is reviewed every year; check the current figure on the HMRC website).
- If you are VAT-registered, you must include your VAT number on every invoice and apply the appropriate rate (standard, reduced or zero).

For cross-border sales to the EU, treat them as exports. For services sold to EU business customers, the place-of-supply rules generally place the transaction in the customer's country, and the customer accounts for VAT under reverse charge in the same way as before.

## Currency and language

In cross-border invoicing, two extra conventions help:

- Always include the ISO currency code (USD, EUR, GBP) next to the total.
- Issue invoices in English in addition to (or instead of) your local language when the client is in a different country. Most accounting departments worldwide can process an English invoice; the reverse is not always true.

## Common cross-border pitfalls

- Forgetting to include the client's VAT number when invoicing a VAT-registered EU business; this can disqualify the invoice from reverse charge and force you to charge local VAT.
- Using a date format that the client misreads, leading to incorrect due-date computation.
- Quoting amounts in a local currency without an ISO code, so the client transfers funds in the wrong currency.
- Failing to note the place of supply for digital services to EU consumers (the rules around "electronically supplied services" and one-stop-shop registration deserve their own conversation with an accountant).

If you regularly invoice across borders, set up a one-hour annual review with a local accountant; the cost is usually trivial compared with a single mishandled VAT case.
